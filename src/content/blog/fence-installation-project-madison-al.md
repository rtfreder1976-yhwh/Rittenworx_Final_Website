Most of what we post is guides and cost breakdowns. This one's different — a walkthrough of an actual fence project in Madison, start to finish, and the decisions that went into it.

## The Starting Point

The homeowner's old fence line had done its job for years, but it was showing every one of them: posts leaning at different angles, a gate that no longer latched, and boards that had gone gray and splintery. It wasn't one dramatic failure — it was a dozen small ones adding up, the kind of wear that happens gradually enough that you stop noticing it until you really look.

The property line also needed to hold a dog in reliably, which ruled out patch repairs on a fence that was already failing in more than one spot.

## What We Changed

Rather than repairing individual sections, we pulled the old line and started fresh:

*   **New posts, set deeper.** Every post went into a fresh footing below the frost line, rather than reusing the old holes — reused holes are exactly why the old posts had started leaning in the first place.
*   **Pressure-treated frame, cedar-style board spacing.** Structural lumber that resists rot below the soil line, with board spacing tight enough to hold a dog but still let air move through so the wood doesn't stay wet after rain.
*   **A gate that actually latches.** Self-closing hinges and a positive-latch mechanism, so it's not something the homeowner has to double-check every time.

## Why This Approach Holds Up

A fence that's built right doesn't fail evenly — it fails at the posts first, almost always. Setting new posts properly (not just replacing the boards on old, compromised posts) is the difference between a fence that's solid for the next 15-20 years and one that starts leaning again in three.
