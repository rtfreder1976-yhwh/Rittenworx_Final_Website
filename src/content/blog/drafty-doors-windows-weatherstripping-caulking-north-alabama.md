If one room in your house is always colder than the rest, or your heat pump runs nonstop on cold nights, there's a good chance air is getting in around a door or window. The fixes are usually small and inexpensive. The hard part is finding where the air is coming from.

## How to Find Drafts

Pick a cold, windy evening and try these:

- **The light test.** At night, turn on the lights inside and look at exterior doors from outside, or turn them off inside and look for daylight around doors during the day. Any light you can see is a gap.
- **The dollar bill test.** Close a door or window on a dollar bill. If it slides out with no resistance, the seal isn't touching.
- **The incense or candle test.** Hold a lit incense stick near door edges, window frames, outlets on exterior walls, and attic hatches. Where the smoke wavers sideways, air is moving.
- **The hand test.** Simple, but it works: run the back of your hand slowly along the edges of doors and windows.

## The Most Common Fixes

### Worn or missing door sweep

The sweep is the rubber or brush strip on the bottom of an exterior door. They wear out, tear, or get knocked loose. If you can see light under the door, replacing the sweep is usually the fix. It's one of the quickest jobs we do.

### Compressed weatherstripping

The foam or rubber strip around the door frame gets flattened over time and stops sealing. Replacement weatherstripping comes in many profiles; the key is matching the kerf (slot) style your door frame uses.

### Door that doesn't close tight

Sometimes the weatherstripping is fine, but the door itself has sagged or the strike plate has shifted, so it doesn't press against the seal. North Alabama's humidity swings make wood doors swell and shrink through the year. Tightening hinges with longer screws, shimming a hinge, or adjusting the strike plate often fixes the draft and the sticking at the same time.

### Cracked exterior caulk

Look at the caulk where window and door trim meets the siding or brick. If it's cracked, shrunk, or missing, cold air and rainwater both get in. Old caulk needs to be fully removed before new caulk goes on; caulking over old caulk doesn't last.

### Gaps around pipes and wires

Where plumbing, cable, and electrical lines pass through exterior walls, there's often an unsealed gap. Low-expansion foam or caulk closes these up.

## When It's More Than a Draft

Some problems need more than a seal:

- **Rotted door frames or sills.** If the bottom of the door frame is soft, new weatherstripping won't stop the air. The rotted wood needs to be cut out and replaced.
- **Foggy double-pane windows.** Fog between the panes means the seal has failed. That's a window replacement question, not a caulking one.
- **Cold air from outlets everywhere.** That usually points to missing insulation in the walls, which is a bigger project.
