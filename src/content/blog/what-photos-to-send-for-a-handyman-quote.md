A lot of the jobs we do start with a text message and a couple of photos. When the photos show the right things, that's often enough to give you an estimate the same day, or at least to know what to bring when we come out. When they don't, it turns into five rounds of "can you send one from farther back?"

Here's what to send.

## The Three Shots That Work for Almost Any Job

1. **One wide shot.** Step back far enough to show the whole wall, door, or area and what's around it. This tells us scale, access, and whether furniture or a ladder setup is going to be an issue.
2. **One close-up of the problem.** The crack, the rot, the stain, the gap. Get close enough that it's in focus.
3. **One shot with something for scale.** A tape measure laid next to the damage is ideal. A hand, a pen, or a dollar bill works too.

Then add a sentence or two: what's going on, how long it's been happening, and whether there's anything else on your list while we're there.

## Job-by-Job: What Else Helps

### Drywall holes and cracks

- A close-up with a tape measure across the hole
- A wide shot of the wall, so we can see whether the patch will be in a spot that catches light
- If there's water staining, a photo of whatever is above it: a bathroom, an attic, a roof line

Water stains matter. Patching drywall without fixing the leak behind it just means patching it again. We cover that in more detail in [what to do when you find water damage behind drywall](/blog/what-to-do-when-you-find-water-damage-behind-drywall).

### TV mounting

- A wide shot of the wall where the TV will go
- The TV size, and the mount if you already have one (a photo of the box is fine)
- Whether the wall is drywall, brick, or stone, and whether you want the cables hidden
- If it's over a fireplace, a photo of the mantel and the wall above it

More on what's involved on our [TV mounting page](/services/tv-mounting).

### Doors that stick or won't latch

A **short video** is better than photos here. Open and close the door once, slowly, and let the camera see where it rubs and whether the latch lines up. Also take a close-up of the top hinge. A loose top hinge is the most common cause, and it usually shows. See our [door repair page](/services/door-repair) for the usual fixes.

### Painting

- A wide shot of each room or wall you want painted
- Rough room dimensions or ceiling height if they're not standard
- Close-ups of any peeling, water stains, or patched areas that need work first

For exterior painting, add a photo of each side of the house and close-ups of any soft or rotted trim. Paint doesn't fix rot.

### Flooring

- The room from a doorway, showing the whole floor
- Where the new floor will meet other floors (doorways, transitions to tile or carpet)
- The room's rough dimensions
- Close-ups of any soft spots, buckling, or squeaks

### Deck and fence repair

- A wide shot of the whole deck or fence run
- Close-ups of the base of any posts that look dark, soft, or leaning
- For a deck, a photo from underneath if you can safely get one, since that's where the structural problems hide

### Gutters

- A photo of the roof edge from the ground on each side with gutters
- Close-ups of anywhere water overflows, sags, or pulls away from the fascia
- How many stories the house is

## What a Photo Can't Tell Us

Photos get you a fast answer on a lot of jobs, but not all of them. Anything structural, anything where water has been getting in for a while, and anything where the damage might go further than what's visible usually needs eyes on it. If we look at your photos and think that's the case, we'll say so and set up a time to come out, rather than guess at a number and change it later.
