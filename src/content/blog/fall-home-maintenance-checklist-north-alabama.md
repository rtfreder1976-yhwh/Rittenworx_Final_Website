Fall is the easiest season to work on a house in North Alabama. The humidity finally breaks, the afternoons are mild, and you have a window of a few weeks before the leaves come down and the first cold snap arrives.

It's also the season where skipping a couple of small jobs turns into a much bigger repair by February. Here's what's actually worth walking around and checking before winter.

## 1. Gutters — But Wait for the Leaves

The most common mistake is cleaning gutters in September. Around here the oaks and maples don't finish dropping until well into November, and the pines shed needles most of the year. Clean too early and you'll be doing it again.

What you can do now is check the hardware while the gutters are still empty enough to see:

*   **Look for sagging sections.** A gutter that dips in the middle is holding standing water, which means the hangers have pulled loose or the fascia behind them has gone soft.
*   **Check where the downspouts let out.** If water is dumping right against the foundation, that's the single most damaging thing happening to your house every time it rains.
*   **Look at the fascia board itself.** Dark streaks or soft spots under the gutter line mean water has been getting behind it.

Then do the actual cleaning once the trees are bare. More on that in our [gutter cleaning](/services/gutter-cleaning) rundown.

## 2. Exterior Caulk and Paint

North Alabama summers are hard on caulk. Months of heat and humidity followed by a dry fall causes joints to shrink and split — usually around window trim, door casings, and where siding meets brick.

Walk the perimeter of the house and look at every seam. If you can slide a fingernail into a gap, water can get in too. Fall is a good time to deal with it because temperatures are still in the range most exterior caulks and paints want, and you're sealing things up before winter rain rather than after.

Peeling or chalky paint on south- and west-facing walls is worth noting now even if you don't plan to repaint until spring. Bare wood left through a wet winter is a lot more work to prep later. We cover the warning signs in our post on [what to do when exterior paint starts peeling](/blog/what-to-do-when-exterior-paint-starts-peeling).

## 3. Doors and Weatherstripping

The Tennessee Valley usually sees its first real freeze somewhere around mid- to late November, and drafty doors get noticed the same week the heat kicks on.

*   Close each exterior door on a strip of paper. If it pulls out with no resistance, the weatherstripping isn't sealing.
*   Look at the bottom of exterior door jambs and garage service doors — that's where rot starts, because it's where splashback hits.
*   Check that storm doors latch without being forced. A door that has to be shoved is usually a hinge or strike plate that's shifted, not a door that's warped.

These are small [handyman jobs](/services/general-handyman) individually, but they're the ones that make a noticeable difference in how the house feels in January.

## 4. Interior Cracks and Settling

Our clay soils swell in wet weather and shrink in dry spells, and houses move with them. Fall is when a lot of homeowners first notice hairline cracks above door frames or at drywall corners.

Most seasonal cracking is cosmetic — the same crack opening and closing slightly through the year. What's worth a closer look:

*   Cracks wider than about an eighth of an inch.
*   Cracks that run diagonally from the corner of a door or window opening.
*   Doors that have started to stick or latch badly at the same time cracks appeared.
*   Any crack that keeps coming back after being patched.

That pattern can point to something structural rather than cosmetic, and it's worth having somebody look before you patch over it. Straightforward seasonal cracks are a normal [drywall repair](/services/drywall).

## 5. Decks, Steps and Handrails

Before the wet months, put weight on things. Grab handrails and pull. Step hard on the tread nearest the ground — that's the one that stays damp longest and fails first. Look at the base of any post that sits in or near soil.

Rotten exterior wood doesn't get better over winter, and it's a lot cheaper to replace one stair stringer in October than to rebuild a set of steps in March.

## A Realistic Order of Operations

If you only get to a few of these, this is roughly the order that saves the most trouble:

1. Fix anything actively letting water in — failed caulk, a gutter dumping at the foundation, rotten trim.
2. Seal up drafts before the first cold snap.
3. Clean gutters once the leaves are down.
4. Note cosmetic items for spring.
