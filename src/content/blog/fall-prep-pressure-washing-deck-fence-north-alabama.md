September is the window. The heat's finally breaking, the humidity's dropping, and you've still got a stretch of dry, mild weather before winter makes outdoor work miserable. If you've been putting off anything on the outside of the house, this is the month to get to it — not April, when everyone else is calling at once.

Three things worth doing now, in order.

## 1. Pressure Wash Before You Do Anything Else

Every exterior project starts cleaner. If you're planning to stain a deck, paint trim, or just want the siding to stop looking green in the shady spots, washing it first is what makes the next step actually work — paint and stain don't hold on top of mildew and pollen film.

A few things worth knowing before you rent a machine and do it yourself:

- **Siding gets low pressure, not full blast.** Vinyl and Hardie board should be soft-washed — low PSI plus a cleaning solution — not hit at full pressure. Full pressure can drive water behind the siding or crack it.
- **Your roof should never see a pressure washer at all.** High pressure strips the granules off asphalt shingles, which is the layer that protects them from UV and weather. If you've got black streaking on the roof, that's algae, and it needs a soft wash, not a blast.
- **Concrete can take it.** Driveways and walkways are the one place where higher pressure actually helps — it's usually what it takes to lift oil stains and ground-in dirt.

## 2. Check the Deck Now, Not When Someone's Standing on It

We've written before about [spring deck inspection](/blog/spring-deck-and-exterior-trim-repair-guide-madison-al), but fall matters just as much: whatever's wrong now sits through the winter and gets worse before you notice it again in spring.

The two spots that matter most:

- **The ledger board**, where the deck bolts to the house. It's usually hidden behind the rim joist, so rot here doesn't show from the top. If the deck feels like it shifts slightly when a few people are standing on it, that's your sign to look closer.
- **The posts**, at ground level where they've been sitting in wet mulch or grass all summer. Push a screwdriver into the base of each one — if it sinks in easily, that's soft wood, not solid wood.

Board replacement, restaining, and rail tightening are quick fall fixes. Structural repair — sistering a joist, replacing a post — takes longer and is a lot easier to schedule before winter than after a February ice storm makes your yard unworkable. ## 3. Walk Your Fence Line

Fences don't fail all at once — they fail post by post, usually starting at the base where wood sits against damp soil the longest. Fall is a good time to walk the line and look for:

- Posts that wobble or lean when you push on them
- Rot starting at the bottom few inches of any post
- Gates that have started dragging or won't latch cleanly
- Panels that came loose over the summer and never got fixed

A single rotted post is a cheap fix. Three or four rotted posts on the same run usually means the whole section needs to come out. Catching it now, while the ground is still workable and before a winter storm takes down a weak section, is the difference between a repair and a rebuild. Details on what that looks like are on our [fence installation & repair page](/services/fence-installation).

## The Short Version

If you only do one thing this month: walk the outside of your house with a screwdriver and a critical eye. Push on deck posts, check fence posts at the base, and look at what pressure washing would actually fix versus what needs real repair first. Wash after you repair, not before — no point cleaning wood you're about to replace.
