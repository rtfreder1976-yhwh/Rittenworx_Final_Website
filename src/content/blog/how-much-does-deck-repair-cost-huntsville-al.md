"How much to fix my deck?" is one of those questions that has no single answer until someone's actually looked at it, because deck repair pricing depends almost entirely on where the damage is — not just how much of it there is. A deck with ten cosmetic boards to swap can cost less than a deck with one bad ledger board. Here's why.

## The Ledger Board Changes Everything

The ledger board is the piece that bolts your deck to the house, and it's the single most expensive repair when it fails — not because the board itself is pricey, but because of what it takes to get to it. It's usually hidden behind the rim joist or siding, so replacing it means partially disassembling the deck's connection point, checking the framing behind it for water damage, and rebuilding that joint correctly so the deck is actually attached, not just resting against the house. If your deck feels like it shifts slightly when a few people stand on it, this is usually why, and it's worth having checked before it gets worse.

## Joist and Post Repair Is the Next Tier Up

Sistering a joist (bolting a new piece of lumber alongside a weakened one) or replacing a support post costs more than surface repairs but less than ledger board work, since it's more straightforward access. How many joists or posts need attention matters a lot here — one soft post found early is a quick fix; rot that's spread to three or four support points usually means more of the substructure needs attention.

## Board Replacement Is the Cheapest Real Repair

Swapping individual cracked, cupped, or splintering boards is priced per board, and it's the most affordable category of deck repair by far — as long as the framing underneath is sound. This is also where wood vs. composite decking matters for your wallet long-term: pressure-treated pine boards cost less upfront and are easy to replace one at a time as they wear. Composite costs more per board but won't rot, so you're not budgeting for periodic board swaps the way you would with wood.

## Staining and Sealing Is Maintenance, Not Repair — But Skipping It Gets Expensive

A wood deck that hasn't been stained or sealed in 3+ years is usually starting to gray, absorb water instead of shedding it, and splinter. Staining costs far less than what you'll eventually pay in board replacement if you let a deck sit unprotected through multiple North Alabama summers and winters. Think of it as the cheapest repair you can do, because it's the one that prevents the expensive ones.

## When Repair Costs More Than Rebuilding

If rot has spread across the ledger board, multiple joists, and several posts, you're paying labor to fix each problem individually — and that adds up faster than most homeowners expect. At a certain point, a new deck built to current framing standards costs about the same as chasing repairs across a structure that's failing in more than one place, and you end up with something that's solid everywhere instead of solid in patches. We'll always tell you honestly which side of that line you're on rather than upselling a rebuild you don't need.

## What a Real Inspection Checks

1. Ledger board condition (the most common hidden failure point)
2. Joists and posts, probed with a screwdriver — soft wood sinks in easily
3. Railing and stair stability
4. Overall board condition and spacing

You get a written estimate after that walkthrough — a clear repair-or-rebuild recommendation with a real number, not a guess from the driveway.
